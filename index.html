Legally Required Finished Product Testing (Table 7) — cfu/g
How to read n / c / m / M:
n = number of sample units taken (always 5 here)  ·  c = max units allowed to fall between m and M before batch fails  ·  m = standard/target — aim to be below this  ·  M = absolute ceiling — ANY unit above M = automatic fail regardless of others.

Example — Meat Preparations E. coli (the 5000 rule): Take 5 units. Up to 2 may fall between 500–5000 cfu/g (the amber zone). If 3+ fall in that zone OR any single unit exceeds 5000 → batch fails outright. All 5 under 500 = compliant. The "limit" people quote as 5000 is M — the ceiling. The real target (m) is 500.

Genetics & Breed Selection
Commercial broiler genetics — Ross 308, Cobb 500 — and how breed choice dictates everything downstream
⚡
Must Know — Chapter 01
  • Ross 308 & Cobb 500 are the two dominant UK commercial broiler breeds
  • Target live weight: 2.0–2.8kg at 35–42 days for standard whole bird
  • FCR (Feed Conversion Ratio): target 1.55–1.70 kg feed per kg liveweight
  • Breed choice is set by integrator — factory has no influence; impacts yield daily
  • Breast meat yield ~22–26% of live weight — key factory yield driver
  • Wooden Breast & White Striping are breed-linked myopathies — directly impact QA line
  • Kill age directly determines final piece weight bands (4oz / 6oz / 8oz)
  • Red Tractor mandates UK-born, UK-reared — breed genetics must comply with scheme
Commercial Breed Landscape Live Production
  • Two primary genetic lines dominate UK commercial poultry:
  • Ross 308 (Aviagen) — most widely used in UK; standard for most major retailer supply chains including QSR accounts
  • Cobb 500 (Cobb-Vantress) — slightly higher breast meat yield in some trial conditions; used by several UK integrators
  • Hubbard / Ranger Gold — slower-growing alternatives mandated by RSPCA Assured and BCC; longer grow cycles, lower stocking density
  • Genetic companies do not sell birds to farms — they sell fertile hatching eggs to hatcheries via a grandparent/parent/commercial flock pyramid
  • Grandparent flock (GP) → Parent flock (PS) → Commercial flock (broiler) — three generations before factory bird
  • Any disease at GP level cascades down all commercial production — biosecurity at breeder level is critical
  • Breed selection is determined by the integrator (e.g., the processing company or farming division); individual farms within a supply chain run the same breed for uniformity

Breast Meat Myopathies — QA Critical Critical
  • Wooden Breast (WB): Myopathy causing pectoral muscle to become firm, pale, and ridged — hardness detectable at deboning
  • Cause: Rapid muscle growth exceeding vascular oxygen delivery capacity — directly linked to fast-growing genetics
  • Factory impact: Texture failure, reduced water-holding capacity, drip loss increase, consumer complaint trigger
  • QA action: Tactile check at deboning — reject if rigid/woody; log and trend per flock/farm
  • White Striping (WS): White fat streaks running parallel to muscle fibres on breast surface
  • Graded: Mild / Moderate / Severe — severe WS associated with reduced protein, increased fat content
  • Retailer implication: Some specifications cap % of severe WS product; must be tracked at QA point
  • Spaghetti Meat: Breast fillet loses structural integrity — pulls apart in fine fibres
  • Emerging myopathy; particularly problematic for whole fillet retail SKUs
  • All three myopathies increase with kill weight — heavier birds at older kill age = higher incidence
  • Trend by farm/flock: If specific farms show elevated myopathy rates, feed programme or kill age may need adjustment; Technical team reports to supply chain
Breed Choice & Retailer/Customer Compliance Compliance
  • Standard commercial supply (QSR, mainstream retail): Ross 308 or Cobb 500 — fast-growing, high yield, no breed restriction beyond Red Tractor minimum
  • RSPCA Assured supply: Must use slower-growing breeds — Hubbard Ranger Gold or equivalent; kill age typically 56+ days; lower stocking density; natural light

Hatchery Operations
Incubation, hatching, chick quality, vaccination and day-one biosecurity — the bridge between genetics and the farm
⚡
Must Know — Chapter 02
  • Incubation period: 21 days total — setter (18 days) + hatcher (3 days)
  • Setter temp: 37.5–37.8°C; hatcher: 36.9–37.2°C
  • Hatchability target: >85% of set eggs hatching viable chicks
  • In-ovo or day-of-hatch vaccination covers Marek's, HVT, Gumboro, IBV
  • Vertical transmission of Salmonella possible via egg — hatchery hygiene critical
  • Chick quality score (first 24h) predicts flock performance and mortality
  • Transport from hatchery to farm must be completed within 36 hours of hatch
  • Hatchery is a biosecurity boundary — all staff, trays, equipment separately controlled
Incubation Process Process
  • Stage 1 — Setter (Days 1–18): Eggs placed in multi-stage setters; temperature, humidity, and CO₂ precisely controlled
  • Temperature: 37.5–37.8°C; Relative Humidity: 52–56%; CO₂: <0.5%
  • Eggs turned automatically every 45–60 minutes to prevent embryo adhesion to shell membrane
  • Candling at Day 7–10: infertile and early dead eggs identified and removed (reduces contamination risk)
  • Stage 2 — Hatcher (Days 18–21): Eggs transferred to separate hatcher trays; turning stops; humidity raised
  • Temperature: 36.9–37.2°C; RH raised to 65–75% to facilitate shell pipping
  • Chicks pip (break shell) at ~Day 20.5; full hatch at Day 21
  • Hatch window: Ideally all chicks hatch within 6 hours of each other — extended windows indicate egg storage or incubation problems
  • Residual yolk sac: Provides 72 hours of nutrition post-hatch — gives time for transport and farm placement before first feed
Vaccination at Hatchery Disease Prevention
  • In-ovo vaccination (Day 18 transfer): Automated machine injects vaccine directly into air cell
  • Marek's Disease (MD) — HVT/Rispens combination; lifetime immunity essential
  • Gumboro (IBD) — protects bursa of Fabricius; immunosuppression prevention
  • Infectious Bronchitis Virus (IBV) — respiratory protection; strain selection varies by region
  • Day-of-hatch spray vaccination: Chicks in hatcher receive fine aerosol spray
  • Newcastle Disease (ND) — live attenuated vaccine; legal requirement under UK law
  • IBV booster if in-ovo not used
  • Vaccine cold chain: Must be maintained at 2–8°C from manufacturer to point of use; broken cold chain = vaccine failure = flock disease risk
  • Vaccine records: Batch number, expiry, time of use — mandatory for Red Tractor and retailer assurance audits; records available to Technical team on request
Chick Quality Assessment QA
  • Pasgar Score or equivalent used to assess chick quality at hatch:
  • Vitality — response to stimulus (active vs lethargic)
  • Eyes — fully open, bright
  • Navel — clean, fully closed; open navel = infection risk = reject
  • Legs — correct pigmentation, stand straight; splayed legs = leg weakness at kill age
  • Beak — aligned, no cross-beak
  • Uniformity — consistent size within tray; rejects removed
  • Second-quality chicks (culls): humanely destroyed at hatchery — not placed on farm
  • Chick quality directly predicts: day 7 mortality, final FCR, uniformity at kill — poor chick quality shows up weeks later as factory yield variation
  • Salmonella Enteritidis (SE) testing: Parent flock fluff swabs taken at hatchery; positive result = legal obligation to destroy/divert all commercial eggs from that PS flock
Biosecurity at Hatchery Mandatory
  • Hatchery is a high-risk biosecurity zone: Incoming eggs are unvaccinated and susceptible; contamination spreads to thousands of chicks simultaneously
  • Fumigation: Historically formaldehyde; now replaced by hydrogen peroxide fogging systems in UK hatcheries — egg surface disinfection mandatory
  • Traffic flow: Strict clean/dirty separation — incoming egg lorries vs outgoing chick delivery trucks must not cross paths
  • Staff hygiene: Sector-specific PPE, footbaths, shower-in policies for entry to setter/hatcher rooms
  • Pest control: Hatchery environment particularly attractive to rodents and wild birds — rodent bait stations mandatory; all drainage points proofed
  • Meconium (chick faeces): Generated in large quantities in hatcher; Salmonella risk if chicks are held too long — dispatch timing critical

Chapter 03
Live Production & Flock Management
Broiler house environment, stocking density, ventilation, lighting programmes, and daily flock husbandry from day-old to catch
⚡
Must Know — Chapter 03
  • UK legal stocking density: max 33 kg/m²; up to 39 kg/m² with welfare monitoring scheme
  • Broiler house temperature day 1: 30–33°C; reduces ~0.5°C/day to ~18–20°C at kill age
  • Ammonia limit in shed: <20 ppm — above this causes respiratory damage, eye lesions
  • CO₂ in shed: <3000 ppm — higher = inadequate ventilation = immune suppression
  • Litter quality directly impacts Campylobacter levels at slaughter — wet litter = pathogen reservoir
  • Water consumption drop >20% in 24h = early disease/welfare alert requiring vet notification
  • Mortality >3.5% triggers mandatory vet investigation and regulatory notification
  • Thinning (partial depopulation) allowed under UK law; each thin = biosecurity event
Broiler House Environment Process Parameters
  • Temperature management — critical in first week:
  • Day 1: 30–33°C at chick level; reduce by ~0.5°C per day
  • Week 2 target: ~26–28°C; Week 4+: 18–22°C depending on stocking density
  • Temperature measured at bird level — roof thermometers do not reflect chick experience
  • Chick behaviour is the primary indicator: spreading = too hot; huddling under heater = too cold; spreading evenly = optimal
  • Ventilation systems:
  • Minimum ventilation (winter): maintains air quality, removes moisture and ammonia without chilling birds
  • Tunnel ventilation (summer): high-speed air movement to provide evaporative cooling at high temperatures
  • Transition ventilation: automatic control between modes based on house temp and setpoint
  • Lighting programmes:
  • Standard: 23 hours light / 1 hour dark (first week); stepped reduction to 18/6 after 7 days
  • Purpose: stimulate feeding behaviour early; dark period reduces leg problems and sudden death syndrome
  • RSPCA Assured requires minimum 8 hours dark and 6 lux minimum — significant change from standard
  • Litter management:
  • Fresh wood shavings or straw at placement; 5–10 cm depth
  • Wet litter = ammonia production + footpad dermatitis + Campylobacter proliferation
  • Litter condition scored weekly; action required if wet patches develop (leaking drinkers, poor ventilation)
Stocking Density & Welfare Legislation Legal
  • Welfare of Farmed Animals (England) Regulations 2007 (Broiler Directive implementation):
  • Baseline maximum: 33 kg/m² stocking density
  • Can increase to 39 kg/m² if farmer operates an approved welfare monitoring programme (daily mortality/culls recording, gait scoring, footpad scoring)
  • Can increase to 42 kg/m² with documented satisfactory flock-level welfare results over previous three flocks
  • RSPCA Assured maximum: 30 kg/m² — lower than legal standard; requires natural light minimum 20 lux
  • Better Chicken Commitment: maximum 30 kg/m² combined with slower-growing breed and enrichment requirement
  • Welfare monitoring records are audited by Red Tractor, RSPCA, and retailer assurance schemes — Technical team may be asked to verify data during audits
  • Footpad Dermatitis (FPD) scoring: Birds scored at slaughter by OV/MHI at factory; poor FPD score results can lead to withdrawal of higher density permission for that farm
Daily Flock Monitoring & Early Warning Indicators Operational
  • Mortality tracking: Dead and culled birds counted and removed daily; entered onto flock record system
  • First-week mortality >1% = significant concern; vet notification typically required
  • Mortality spike mid-flock = disease event; farm contacts integrator vet immediately
  • Water consumption monitoring: Automated flow meters on nipple drinker lines
  • Healthy birds consume ~1.7–2x water vs feed intake by weight
  • Drop >20% in 24 hours = alarm trigger; causes include drinker blockage, disease, temperature stress
  • Feed consumption monitoring: Automatic feed systems log daily delivery; any significant drop cross-referenced with water consumption
  • Gait scoring: Weekly assessment of walking ability (0–5 scale); score >3 = welfare concern; >5% birds at score 3+ triggers action
  • Thinning (partial depopulation): Removal of a proportion of birds mid-cycle to reduce stocking density for remaining birds
  • Thinned birds are slaughtered; remaining flock continues to target kill weight
  • Each thinning = biosecurity event; trucks bringing new birds must be excluded during this period; Campylobacter status of remaining flock re-evaluated

Feed, Nutrition & Growth Economics
Feed accounts for ~70% of live production cost — understanding FCR, ingredient composition and growth phases is fundamental to yield and cost

Must Know — Chapter 04

Key Ingredients & Their Roles Nutritional Science
  • Wheat / Maize: Primary energy source (~40–50% of diet); provides starch for ME; maize gives yellow skin pigmentation (UK preference is yellow-skin birds for many QSR accounts)
  • Soya bean meal: Primary protein source; provides essential amino acids (lysine, methionine); GMO-free sourcing required for RSPCA and some retailer standards
  • Lysine (synthetic): First limiting amino acid in poultry; addition ensures protein efficiency without excess crude protein
  • Methionine (synthetic): Critical for feathering, muscle growth and liver function; added to all commercial diets

  • Probiotics / Organic acids: Added to starter feeds in ABF programmes to support gut microbiome in absence of antibiotic growth promoters
  • Xanthophyll pigments: Derived from marigold extract; added to enhance skin/fat yellow colour for specific markets

  • FCR formula: Total feed consumed (kg) ÷ Total liveweight gain (kg)
  • Industry standard targets: 1.55–1.70 for standard commercial Ross/Cobb at 35–42 days
  • Factors worsening FCR: Disease, poor ventilation, temperature extremes, poor litter, high mortality, feed wastage, poor water quality
  • EPEF (European Production Efficiency Factor): Combined performance index = (Liveability% × Avg daily gain) ÷ (FCR × 10); higher = better; target typically >350

Flock Health, Disease & Biosecurity
Key poultry pathogens, notifiable diseases, and the biosecurity framework that prevents farm-to-factory disease transmission
⚡
Must Know — Chapter 05
  • Avian Influenza (AI) is a Notifiable Disease — legal duty to report to APHA within 24h
  • AI H5N1 / H5N8 — HPAI strains cause mass mortality; triggered national restrictions 2020–2024
  • Campylobacter jejuni/coli — most common bacterial cause of human food poisoning in UK
  • Salmonella Enteritidis (SE) & Typhimurium (ST) — zoonotic; legal duty to test and control

  • Infectious Bursal Disease (Gumboro) — immunosuppression; increases susceptibility to all other pathogens
  • Farm biosecurity failure = factory pathogen load increase = consumer illness risk

Notifiable Diseases — Legal Framework Mandatory Reporting
  • Avian Influenza (AI): Any suspicion must be reported to APHA within 24 hours; farm placed under movement restriction immediately
  • HPAI (Highly Pathogenic) — H5N1, H5N8: 90%+ flock mortality; national control zone established; all poultry in 3km Protection Zone slaughtered
  • LPAI (Low Pathogenic) — may cause only mild respiratory signs; still notifiable
  • Factory impact: Any bird from a restricted premises cannot be slaughtered for human consumption; incoming load verification at factory gate is a legal control
  • Newcastle Disease (ND / PMV-1): Highly contagious respiratory/neurological; controlled by mandatory vaccination; unvaccinated UK flocks susceptible
  • Infectious Bronchitis (IB): Respiratory — not notifiable but economically devastating; reduces egg production in layers; affects carcass quality in broilers
  • Campylobacter: Not notifiable at farm level — but the FSA operates a compulsory testing programme at factory; results are published publicly against factory name
  • Salmonella (SE and ST specifically): Legal duty under National Control Programme (NCP) to test flocks; positive result → mandatory slaughter and destruction (SE) or heat treatment diversion (ST)
Campylobacter — The Primary Factory-Relevant Pathogen Critical
  • Campylobacter jejuni / coli: Gram-negative, microaerophilic curved rod; most common cause of human foodborne illness in UK (~280,000 cases/year)
  • Farm reservoir: Colonises broiler intestinal tract typically from 2–3 weeks of age; horizontal spread within shed is rapid; not all farms colonised
  • Factory amplification risk: Scalding and plucking can spread from colonised to clean birds; evisceration rupture spreads faecal contamination; chilling reduces but does not eliminate
  • FSA Campylobacter testing programme: Mandatory neck skin swab samples taken from random flocks at slaughter; results reported quarterly; factory-level data published
  • Target: <1000 cfu/g (most probable number) on neck skin; highest contamination category (>1000 cfu/g) should be <10% of flocks tested
  • Intervention points in factory: Rapid surface chilling of carcass (most effective intervention); steam pasteurisation systems; decontamination cabinet; scald temperature management
  • Thinning risk: Farm thinning disturbs litter and dramatically increases Campylobacter spread; thinned birds are highest risk — factories use this information from the farm movement document
Farm Biosecurity Framework Red Tractor / DEFRA
  • DEFRA and Red Tractor minimum biosecurity requirements for broiler farms:
  • Visitor logbook — all farm visitors recorded with date, time, previous poultry contact
  • 48-hour stand-down rule — no visitor who has had contact with poultry in prior 48 hours may enter sheds without full disinfection
  • Footbaths at all shed entrances — with approved disinfectant at correct concentration; concentration checked and logged weekly
  • Vehicle disinfection — all vehicles entering farm boundary disinfected; dedicated farm traffic routes
  • Wild bird exclusion — net and physical exclusion from feed stores and water systems; wild bird contact with flock = AI risk
  • Rodent bait plan — documented and maintained; rodents carry Salmonella; bait maps available for audit
  • Pest activity records — monthly inspection by farm or PCO; any evidence of rodent activity in sheds requires immediate corrective action
  • Avian Influenza Prevention Zone (AIZP): DEFRA can declare an AIPZ requiring all poultry to be housed indoors; free-range and organic supply chains severely impacted during these periods
  • Factory verification: Intake approval process must confirm approved farm list is current; any farm under movement restriction must not be accepted; the load rejection process is a legal food safety control

Must Know — Chapter 07
  • Feed withdrawal: 8–12 hours before slaughter — reduces gut fill, lowers contamination risk
  • Water withdrawal: max 12 hours before slaughter — welfare legislation
  • Transport journey time: max 8 hours without water for broilers (UK Regulation)
  • Catching stress = cortisol surge = PSE meat risk and bruising increase
  • DOA (Dead on Arrival) target: <0.1% of live intake; >0.3% = welfare investigation
  • Factory must verify farm load document before unloading begins
  • Module/crate temperature logged on arrival — thermal stress animals require veterinary decision
  • All birds must reach lairage within the legal transport time window
Catching Operations Welfare Regulated
  • Feed withdrawal timing: 8–12 hours before expected slaughter; longer withdrawal = gut empties (reduced contamination) but increases muscle glycogen depletion → risk of dark cutting (DFD) meat
  • Manual vs. mechanical catching: Most UK operations use mechanical catchers (harvester machines); reduces human handling stress per bird
  • Lighting during catching: Blue or red light reduces panic response in birds during catching; welfare benefit
  • Crating/module density: Birds loaded into plastic modules at appropriate density; overcrowding causes thermal stress and suffocation; density varies with ambient temperature
  • Catching crew biosecurity: Catching teams move between multiple farms; potential Campylobacter vector; ideally same crew assigned to same farm batch; footwear and clothing change between farms required
  • Bruising: Primary cause = rough handling, wing fractures from catching, impact in crates; shows as haematoma at processing — traceable back to catching crew by batch; Technical team logs bruising rates per intake farm load
Transport Conditions & Legal Requirements Legal
  • UK Regulation (EC) 1/2005 on Animal Transport (retained in UK law post-Brexit):
  • Journey time: max 8 hours for broilers without water access
  • Space allowance: minimum 160 cm²/kg (adapted by ambient temperature)
  • Temperature inside module: 15–25°C optimal; <5°C or >35°C = welfare emergency
  • Ventilation: lorry design must maintain airflow; side curtains operated per weather conditions
  • Transport documentation (required at factory gate):
  • Farm movement licence (APHA form, or Animal Movement Licence — AML)
  • Flock information document (FID) — carrying farm ID, medication records, Salmonella status, coccidiostat withdrawal confirmation
  • Red Tractor / RSPCA certificate if applicable
  • Driver's certificate of competence (animal transport)
  • Factory action on document failure: Load cannot be unloaded until documentation is verified; if documents indicate restricted movement (disease status) → quarantine holding area; Technical/QA team notified immediately

  • DOA count: Performed at module unloading; dead birds counted per module per farm load
  • DOA rate calculation: (Number dead ÷ Total birds in load) × 100
  • Target: <0.1% of live intake
  • Action level: >0.3% requires formal investigation — welfare team and farm informed
  • Legal threshold: if cumulative DOA patterns suggest systemic welfare failure → APHA/DEFRA notification
  • Cause investigation: Heat stress, overcrowding, rough handling, pre-existing disease, and transport time all contribute; necropsy of DOAs by OV/MHI may be requested
  • Documentation: DOA log is a legal welfare record; retained and available for FSA, retailer, and assurance scheme audit

Lairage Management Legal Requirement
  • Lairage = the holding area where modules/crates are stored after arrival at factory before kill
  • Environmental requirements in lairage:
  • Temperature: 15–25°C maintained; ventilation active; avoid direct sunlight on modules
  • Visual inspection of all modules on arrival — birds visible, breathing, no obvious distress

  • Water provision in lairage: If birds are to be held >4 hours, water provision may be legally required under transport regulation

  • Official Veterinarian (OV): FSA-employed vet stationed at the abattoir; holds ultimate legal authority over kill operations
  • Responsibilities: ante-mortem inspection, post-mortem inspection oversight, slaughter method approval, welfare at slaughter enforcement
  • Powers: suspend slaughter, condemn product, issue Hygiene Improvement Notices, close the line
  • Cannot be overruled by factory management — OV decision is statutory
  • Meat Hygiene Inspector (MHI): FSA-employed technician; works alongside OV on post-mortem line
  • Performs carcass inspection at the evisceration line; marks condemned material
  • Counts and records pathology findings; contributes to flock-level health intelligence
  • Ante-mortem inspection process:
  • OV or delegated MHI inspects live birds in modules before slaughter
  • Any birds showing signs of notifiable disease → held; OV notified; APHA contacted
  • Moribund birds (near death but not dead) — must be killed humanely; cannot enter food chain
  • Farm documentation verified against actual load; any discrepancy → hold and investigate
  • Footpad Dermatitis (FPD) scoring: OV/MHI scores each foot of birds at shackling on 0/1/2 scale; results compiled per farm load and reported back to farm; persistent poor FPD scores can trigger removal of high density permission for that farm
Farm Load Approval Verification — QA Control CCP

  • Approved farm list: Maintained by Technical/QA team; updated as farms enter or exit supply chain; cross-referenced against customer approved supplier list
  • Document check at intake (per QAP Step 2.1):
  • Farm load details: trailer/load number, number of modules, processing line, kill order
  • All approved farms verified as KFC/Red Tractor/customer approved (checked daily prior to kill plan issue)

Chapter 09
Stunning & Slaughter
Electrical water-bath and CAS (gas) stunning — parameters, welfare criteria, slaughter mechanics and the kill CCP
⚡
Must Know — Chapter 09
  • Electrical stunning: inline water bath; min 100mA per bird (UK Welfare Regs)
  • CAS (gas): CO₂ concentration achieves unconsciousness; multi-stage system used in UK
  • Stunning must render bird unconscious before neck cut — failure = serious welfare breach
  • Neck cut must sever both carotid arteries — bilateral cut is standard
  • Bleeding time minimum: 90 seconds before scalder entry
  • CAS multi-stage CO₂ concentrations verified hourly via HMI panel
  • Electrical stimulation (primary) parameters must be recorded at start of shift and every 2 hours
  • Any stun failure = welfare incident = OV notification = line review
Electrical Water Bath Stunning Welfare at Slaughter Regs
  • Process sequence: Birds removed from modules → hung by legs on shackle line → pass through water bath stunner → automated neck cutter → bleed rail
  • Shackling: Birds hung inverted by both legs; shackle line speed determines throughput; speed set to match neck cutter timing
  • Shackling in darkness or dimmed red light reduces bird struggling and wing injuries
  • Electrical parameters (Welfare at Slaughter (England) Regulations 2015 / EC 1099/2009 retained):
  • Minimum current per bird: 100mA (combined head-only + cardiac if used)
  • Frequency: 50Hz (standard UK) — higher frequency (400–1500Hz) reduces blood splash but must achieve equivalent stunning efficacy
  • Voltage: typically 100–350V AC depending on frequency and system design
  • Duration: bird must be in contact with water for sufficient time to receive adequate current
  • Electrical primary stimulation (per QAP 2.3):
  • Stage 1: 1.18% ± 5%; Stage 2: 2.28% ± 5%; Stage 3: 3.38% ± 5%; Stage 4: 4.5% ± 5%; Stage 5: 7.0% ± 150 secs; Exhaust: 20 seconds; Full cycle 360 seconds
  • Settings verified visually at start of shift; concentration readings and cycle graphs accessible from HMI panel; recorded hourly
  • Stun failure indicators: Wing flapping after stunner, open eyes, vocalization on bleed rail — OV notified immediately
Controlled Atmosphere Stunning (CAS / Gas Stunning) CAS System
  • CAS (Controlled Atmosphere Stunning): Birds remain in modules or transferred to gondolas; pass through gas tunnel achieving unconsciousness before shackling
  • CO₂ multi-stage system:
  • Stage 1 (induction): ~40% CO₂ — bird becomes unconscious without distress; CO₂ at this concentration causes narcosis before aversion
  • Stage 2–3: CO₂ concentration increased; cardiac arrest and brain death achieved
  • Exit: bird shackled already unconscious/dead — no shackling stress
  • Welfare advantage over electrical: No live shackling; no water bath entry; significantly reduced wing damage; better blood splash control; mandated by BCC and preferred by RSPCA
  • Monitoring: CO₂ concentration verified at each stage via in-tunnel sensors; logged on HMI panel; hourly checks required; alarm if concentration falls out of specification
  • Argon-based CAS: Some systems use argon or argon/CO₂ mix — higher welfare claim as argon-induced anoxia is not aversive; currently used in premium supply chains
Slaughter — Neck Cut & Bleeding Legal
  • Automatic rotary neck cutter: Standard in UK high-throughput operations; blade height adjusted to bird size to ensure bilateral carotid severance
  • Manual backup cutter: Required on line; operator must cut any bird that automatic cutter misses; missed cuts = live bird entering scalder = welfare offence
  • Bilateral neck cut: Severs both carotid arteries and jugular veins; ensures complete and rapid exsanguination
  • Bleed rail time: Minimum 90 seconds before scalder entry; factory must demonstrate this is achieved at line speed — distance from cut point to scalder entry ÷ line speed = bleed time
  • Blood collection: Trough under bleed rail collects blood; sent for rendering as Category 3 ABP material
  • Missed cut (backup required): Any bird reaching the scalder without effective neck cut = welfare breach and quality failure; red-water scalder (blood in scald tank) indicates poor stun/cut efficacy
  • Operator competence: All slaughter operatives must hold Certificate of Competence (CoC) under UK Welfare at Slaughter legislation; records maintained by factory management; OV verifies

Feather removal mechanics, scald temperature impact on microbiology and meat quality, and the first major pathogen risk point in the factory

Must Know — Chapter 10
  • Scald tank temp: 51–56°C (soft scald); higher = hard scald = skin damage = appearance failure
  • Scald time: typically 120–180 seconds at soft scald temperature
  • Scald water is a major Campylobacter cross-contamination vehicle

Scalding — Process & Microbiology CCP Adjacent
  • Scalding purpose: Loosen feather follicles by denaturing the protein attaching feather to follicle; does NOT remove feathers — that is the plucker's function
  • Soft scald (UK standard): 51–56°C for 120–180 seconds
  • Preserves yellow skin colour and cuticle (outer skin layer); preferred for UK retail market
  • Does not fully denature surface proteins — some Campylobacter reduction but not elimination
  • Hard scald (not standard UK): 58–62°C
  • Removes cuticle completely → white/pale skin; associated with US-style processing
  • Greater pathogen reduction but unacceptable appearance for UK retail
  • Campylobacter risk in scald tank: Highly colonised birds release Campylobacter into scald water; aerosolisation from tank agitation cross-contaminates less-colonised birds
  • Mitigation: continuous fresh water overflow into scald tank dilutes pathogen load; sequential scalding (low contamination birds first) where operationally possible
  • Temperature monitoring (per QAP 2.4): QA verifies and records inline temperature monitoring equipment readings every 2 hours; temperatures outside 51–55°C trigger line slow/stop and engineering notification; maturation minimum 6 hours before re-cut
  • Red water: Blood in scald water from insufficiently bled birds; indicates bleed rail issue; contamination risk; OV informed
Plucking & Finishing Process
  • Plucking machines: Series of rotating drums fitted with rubber fingers; birds pass through multiple plucker sets; each set removes feathers from different body regions
  • Plucker adjustment: Finger gap adjusted to bird size; too tight = bruising and skin tears; too loose = residual feathers
  • Residual feather locations: Wing tips, pin feathers (immature feathers), tail region — removed by singeing (open gas flame) and/or hand finishing operators
  • Singeing: Brief pass through gas flame removes fine hair feathers (filoplumes) without cooking skin; standard step in UK processing
  • Hock cutter: Automated blade removes lower leg (shank + foot) at tibiotarsal joint after plucking; feet are ABP or, in some supply chains, a co-product for export
  • Quality impact of plucking:
  • Skin tears at breast, thigh, wing — visible quality defect; causes: worn plucker fingers, incorrect gap setting, catching injuries
  • Bruising at breast — from catching/shackling stress activating wing beat; dark haematoma
  • QA line checks residual feathers and skin integrity at this point before carcass enters evisceration

Evisceration & Post-Mortem Inspection
Organ removal, viscera inspection, contamination control limits, FSA post-mortem criteria and carcass disposition decisions

Must Know — Chapter 11
  • Evisceration = removal of all internal organs (viscera) from the carcass cavity
  • Vent opener → evisceration spoon/scoop → viscera hung for inspection → organ separation

  • Step 1 — Cloacal opener / vent cutter: Automated blade opens cloaca to allow evisceration scoop entry; incorrect blade depth = intestinal puncture = faecal contamination = line contamination event
  • Step 2 — Neck and crop removal: Crop and neck skin removed; crop rupture risk is high if feed withdrawal is insufficient — underfed birds have full crops that are fragile
  • Step 3 — Evisceration machine: Scoop/spoon enters carcass cavity via cloaca; all viscera drawn out and hung on viscera rack adjacent to carcass
  • Viscera must remain connected to carcass/alongside it for simultaneous OV/MHI inspection (legal requirement under EC 854/2004 retained in UK law)
  • Step 4 — OV/MHI post-mortem inspection:
  • Carcass and viscera inspected simultaneously; OV/MHI checks for pathology, contamination, abnormalities

Post-Mortem Pathology — MHI Condemnation Criteria FSA Statutory
  • Whole carcass condemnation (Total Condemnation — sent to ABP):
  • Systemic infection — generalised septicaemia, polyserositis (airsacculitis + pericarditis + perihepatitis together)
  • Tumours involving multiple organs (Marek's disease lymphoma)
  • Severe cachexia (emaciation) — muscle wasting, no subcutaneous fat

  • Ascites (water belly) — severe fluid accumulation in abdominal cavity
  • Partial condemnation (affected parts removed; remainder passes):
  • Localised abscess — remove affected portion; remainder suitable if systemic spread not suspected
  • Bruising — trim affected area
  • Fracture — remove broken bone area

Common Pathologies Seen at Post-Mortem Diagnostic Reference
  • Airsacculitis: Cloudy, thickened air sacs with yellow exudate; caused by E. coli (colibacillosis), Mycoplasma, or IBV secondary infection; common in UK flocks; partial or total condemnation depending on severity
  • Ascites (Pulmonary Hypertension Syndrome): Yellow watery fluid in abdomen; heart enlargement; caused by rapid growth outpacing cardiovascular capacity; more common in heavier birds; whole condemnation
  • Pericarditis: Fibrinous exudate around heart; usually E. coli or Ornithobacterium rhinotracheale; often accompanies airsacculitis
  • Marek's Disease: Nerve lesions (enlarged sciatic nerve); liver tumours (grey nodules); skin and muscle tumours; partial or total condemnation depending on extent; vaccination failure
  • Cellulitis / Gangrenous dermatitis: Skin necrosis, subcutaneous green-black discolouration; caused by Clostridium perfringens secondary to skin damage; partial condemnation (affected area)
  • Bruising / Haematoma: Dark red/purple areas in muscle or under skin; catching/transport injury; trimmed at partial condemnation
  • Pale Soft Exudative (PSE) meat: Pale, soft, wet-looking breast muscle; caused by pre-slaughter stress, poor stunning, rapid post-mortem pH drop; QA defect rather than condemnation; affects yield and consumer acceptability

Carcass Wash & Pathogen Reduction
Final wash before chilling, decontamination interventions, and the science of surface pathogen reduction in a UK-compliant framework
⚡
Must Know — Chapter 12
  • UK/EU does not permit chemical decontamination washes (e.g., chlorinated water, peracetic acid on carcass) — physical washing only
  • Pre-chill decontamination cabinet (steam pasteurisation) is permitted and used in some UK sites
  • Final carcass wash ≥2 bar removes loose surface contamination and blood
  • Chilling is the primary pathogen reduction step in UK processing — not chemical treatment
  • Post-evisceration sampling for Campylobacter / Salmonella done here for process verification
  • Carcass surface is a cross-contamination risk — any breach means whole line review
  • Inside wash removes residual blood, organ debris — critical for shelf life
  • OPRP (Operational Pre-Requisite Programme) controls wash pressure and water quality
Final Wash System Process
  • Inside-Outside Wash (IORW): High-pressure water jets directed simultaneously into cavity and at outer carcass surface; purpose: remove residual blood clots, organ debris, loose contamination from evisceration
  • Water pressure: ≥2 bar (per QAP specification); automated pressure monitoring with visual indicator and alarm
  • Water temperature: potable (cold); not heated at this stage to avoid protein denaturation of surface
  • Water quality: mains potable supply; E. coli and TVC of process water tested weekly
  • Outside Wash (OW): Additional high-pressure rinse of carcass exterior after final viscera removal; particularly targets neck skin region (Campylobacter monitoring sample site)
  • Pre-Chill Contamination Check (PRCP): QA operator inspects 50 birds per farm load at pre-chill wash point; records any faecal or viscera contamination reaching this stage
  • Post-wash contamination >4% → inform Primary FLM and Shift Manager; carry out Pre-chill contamination check → Neck Cutter, Pre-chill OPRP sprays operational
  • Post-wash contamination >8% → slow line, additional inspection, engineering review of evisceration
UK Position on Decontamination Treatments Regulatory
  • UK and EU (retained law) prohibit chemical decontaminants on poultry carcasses — this distinguishes UK/EU processing from US (which permits chlorine wash, PAA)
  • Physical interventions permitted:
  • High-pressure water washing — standard
  • Steam pasteurisation cabinet — brief steam treatment (typically 10 seconds) at 80–85°C surface temperature; reduces Campylobacter by 1–2 log; approved in UK
  • Hot water cabinet — immersion or spray in water 70°C+ for controlled time; used in some UK sites
  • Ultra-violet treatment of water supply — used for process water disinfection, not direct carcass treatment
  • Why no chemical treatment (EU position): Concern that chemical washing allows lower baseline hygiene standards on the line; EU philosophy is "hygiene at source" — good slaughter hygiene is preferable to chemical remediation at end of process
  • Post-Brexit UK position: UK has retained EU rules; any change would require new UK statutory instrument and FSA risk assessment; no change proposed as of 2024

Chapter 13
Chilling Systems & Temperature Legislation
Air chilling vs immersion chilling, legal temperature requirements, rigor mortis timing, and the connection between chilling and final meat quality
⚡
Must Know — Chapter 13
  • Legal requirement: carcass must reach ≤4°C at deepest point before cutting/boning begins
  • Minimum chill time (per QAP): 2 hours 30 minutes in chill tunnel
  • Chill exit temperature target: <4°C; action if average >4°C → slow line
  • Rigor mortis completes at ~2–4 hours post-slaughter — cutting before rigor = cold shortening risk
  • Air chilling is UK/EU standard for whole birds; immersion chilling may be used in some cutting operations
  • Chilling is the single most effective Campylobacter reduction step in the factory
  • Temperature probe calibration: UKAS-accredited, annually; daily verification required
  • Product held above legal temperature = hold, re-check, release below <4°C or re-work/destroy
Air Chilling — UK Standard Legal Requirement
  • Air chilling process: Carcasses hang on overhead line and pass through a series of refrigerated tunnels; forced cold air circulates around birds
  • Tunnel configuration: Typically 3–4 zones of progressively decreasing temperature
  • Zone 1: ~0°C to +2°C; high airflow; maximum heat removal from hot carcass surface
  • Zone 2–3: ~0°C; sustained cooling of carcass core
  • Exit zone: final temperature verification before cut room entry
  • Legal temperature requirement (Regulation EC 853/2004 retained):
  • Whole poultry carcasses: ≤4°C achieved as soon as possible after post-mortem inspection
  • Giblets: ≤3°C; poultry meat (cut): ≤4°C maintained throughout
  • Monitoring (per QAP 2.9): Inline temperature monitoring in chill tunnel; 10-bird average temperature probe check at exit chiller; UKAS-accredited probe; annually calibrated; daily verification documented; hold product and re-check if average >4°C
  • Air chilling advantages over immersion: No water absorption (no water-added label requirement); better surface microbiological profile; maintains natural skin appearance; required for RSPCA, organic, and premium retail specifications
Rigor Mortis & Meat Quality — The Biochemistry Science
  • Post-mortem, muscle undergoes pH decline: Glycogen → lactic acid via anaerobic glycolysis; pH drops from 7.2 (live) to 5.6–5.8 (rigor)
  • Rigor mortis timing in broilers: Onset ~1–2 hours post-slaughter; full rigor ~3–4 hours; resolution 6–12 hours
  • Cold shortening: If muscle is chilled below 10°C before rigor onset, muscle fibres contract violently producing tough, short-fibred meat; prevented by allowing partial rigor before deep chilling in large muscles
  • Not a major issue in poultry (small muscle mass cools quickly) — more relevant to beef; however, rapid surface chilling at chill entry does not penetrate to core immediately
  • PSE (Pale Soft Exudative) meat: If carcass is stressed pre-slaughter, glycogen depletes rapidly → very fast pH drop → protein denaturation at high muscle temp → pale, soft, wet meat with poor water-holding capacity
  • Indicators: catching stress, long transport, heat stress, poor stunning, electrical stunning at incorrect parameters
  • Factory impact: PSE meat has high drip loss (yield loss), poor texture on cooking, poor customer experience — log and trend by farm/catching crew
  • DFD (Dark Firm Dry) meat: Opposite of PSE; glycogen depleted before slaughter (chronic stress, long feed withdrawal) → pH stays high → dark muscle colour, poor shelf life
  • Dark cutting is rare in poultry but can occur with prolonged pre-slaughter fasting >16 hours

Chapter 14
Portioning, Cut-Up & Yield Management
From whole chilled carcass to finished portions — cut mechanics, weight band specifications, grading, defect classification and yield mathematics
⚡
Must Know — Chapter 14
  • Cutting room temperature: ≤12°C ambient; product ≤4°C throughout
  • Carcass must be in full rigor before cutting — premature cutting causes muscle tearing and yield loss
  • Maturation room (+2°C) holds post-chill birds before cut — allows rigor resolution, improves texture
  • 9-piece cut: 2 wings, 2 drumsticks, 2 thighs, 2 keels, 1 rib (or variant per customer spec)
  • Grading: Green / Amber / Red / Critical — defect system used by QSR customers
  • Weight verification: 10-head checks, hourly — UKAS-calibrated scales mandatory
  • Natural proportion target: minimum 90% — bags must reflect anatomical proportion
  • Piece count and piece type accuracy checked on 5-bag / 10-bag sample per hour
Maturation — The Bridge Between Chill & Cut Process
  • Why maturation matters: Carcass exiting the chiller is in or approaching full rigor — muscle is maximally contracted and tough; cutting at this stage gives poor texture and irregular muscle separation
  • Maturation room: Dedicated chilled holding area (typically +2°C) where birds hang or are racked post-chill before entering the cut room
  • Minimum maturation time for gas flush (CAS-stunned) birds: 6 hours post-stun before packing
  • Electrically-stunned birds: typically 2–4 hours sufficient for adequate rigor resolution
  • Maximum maturation time is governed by shelf-life — longer maturation = reduced shelf life at retail
  • Maturation temperature monitored hourly — product temperature recorded using UKAS-accredited probe; birds outside -1°C to +3°C trigger engineering notification
  • FIFO (First In First Out): Oldest chilled birds must move to cut first; maturation room managed on strict FIFO to prevent shelf-life failures
  • Maturation and gas stun interaction: Gas-stunned (CAS) birds have slower post-mortem pH decline — they require longer maturation than electrically-stunned birds before cutting to achieve same texture outcome
Cut Room Operations & Environment Legal Temperature
  • Legal requirement: Cut room ambient temperature ≤12°C; product temperature ≤4°C maintained at all times during cutting
  • In practice: Cutting halls in high-throughput operations maintained at +6–8°C ambient to balance worker comfort and product temperature compliance
  • Product temperature monitoring: QA checks product temperature at start of shift, hourly, and whenever ambient temperature alarm triggers
  • Line layout (typical whole-bird cut operation):
  • Carcass arrival from maturation → hanging/destacking → individual carcass inspection → automated cut machine or manual cut station → piece separation → grading → weighing → bagging → sealing → coding → casing → palletising
  • Automated cutting machines: High-throughput operations use robotic or semi-automated cutting systems (e.g., Marel, Stork, Baader); set to carcass weight category for consistent cut angles
  • Machine must be reset when carcass weight band changes — incorrect setting = wrong cut angle = defect generation
  • Manual cutting stations: Skilled operatives for non-standard cuts, trimming, finishing; knife hygiene critical — dedicated sanitiser stations, knife exchange protocol, no cross-contamination between product types
  • Knife sterilisation: Operatives required to use knife sterilisers (70°C+ water or UV sanitiser units) between birds; HACCP control for pathogen cross-contamination
9-Piece Cut — Anatomy & Specification QA Reference
  • Standard 9-piece cut (whole bird into 9 portions): 2 wings, 2 drumsticks, 2 thighs, 2 keels (breast portions with rib), 1 back/keel combination — varies by customer specification
  • Wings:
  • Correct cut: circular meat portion (dollar) — target 50mm diameter; 40–65mm acceptable range; minimum 15mm thickness
  • Large dollar (>65mm) — over-cut; Small dollar (<40mm) — under-cut
  • Wing tip missing (partial) = Amber defect; Wing tip totally missing = Red defect
  • Drumsticks:
  • Correct cut: tendons connected to joint; red bone marrow on knuckle; figure-8 shape at cut face visible
  • Short cut at knuckle (below knee): cut too low, no figure 8 visible, no marrow = Amber
  • Long cut at knuckle: hook removed, tendons severed, >9mm of hook remaining below knuckle = Red
  • Thighs:
  • Correct cut: equal portions of backbone remain on each thigh; oyster muscle present bilaterally; thigh joints dislocated (popped) to prevent risk of undercooking at restaurant
  • Mis-cut (short): bone cut too short, no backbone showing = Amber
  • Non-dislocated thigh: oyster muscle joints not popped = Red (undercooking risk)
  • Leaf fat >10g attached, non-dislocated thigh, weight >200g = Critical
  • Keel (breast portion):
  • Correct cut: 2cm meat extending beyond top of sternum; M-shape cross-section; at least 2cm rib on interior side
  • Short keel (<115g) or weight >191g = Red
  • Deep cut (more than 2cm of rib cage exposed at top) = Amber
  • Off-centre/angled cut = Amber; both criteria apply independently per piece
  • Rib:
  • Correct cut: backbone firmly attached to sternum; marrow visible on backbone section; equal portions on each half
  • 4+ broken ribs = Red; rib is slightly weak = Amber
  • Weight: ≥191g = Critical; <115g = Red

Further Processing & Value-Added Products

Must Know — Chapter 15
  • Further processing = any step beyond portioning — marination, breading, cooking, forming
  • Added water / marinade must be declared on label if >5% added ingredient by weight
  • Allergen introduction risk is highest in further processing — control is critical

Marination & Tumbling Process
  • Tumbling: Portions placed in rotating vacuum tumbler with marinade; mechanical action opens muscle fibres and forces marinade penetration; typical cycle 30–60 minutes
  • Marinade ingredients: salt, phosphates (water retention), spices, flavourings, organic acids, sometimes sugars
  • Phosphates (e.g., sodium tripolyphosphate) — increase water-holding capacity; regulated maximum in UK (5g/kg product); must be declared on label as E450 etc.
  • Injection marination: Multi-needle injector drives marinade directly into muscle; faster than tumbling; used for whole breasts or joints; gives higher and more uniform uptake
  • Added water declaration: If final product contains >5% added water/marinade by weight, label must state "Added water (X%)" under UK food labelling law; QA must calculate and verify declaration
  • Marinade pickup %: (Weight after tumbling − Weight before) ÷ Weight before × 100; target varies by product (5–20% typical); verified by QA before packaging
  • Cross-contamination control: Tumbler cleaning and sanitisation between product types; allergen changeover protocol if switching between allergen-containing and allergen-free marinades
Batter, Breading & Coating Allergen Critical
  • Coating sequence: Pre-dust → batter → breader/breadcrumb → frying/par-frying → freezing/chilling
  • Pre-dust: Dry flour coating applied first; improves batter adhesion; commonly wheat flour — major allergen (gluten)
  • Batter: Liquid coating of flour, water, leavening agents, seasoning; applied by batter applicator machine; batter viscosity (Bostwick flow) monitored and adjusted to maintain consistent pickup
  • Breadcrumbs: Panko (Japanese-style large flake), fine, or seasoned crumb applied in rotating drum after batter; texture and colour are key QA parameters
  • Par-frying (tempering): Brief partial fry (typically 45–90 seconds at 185–190°C) to set coating, not fully cook meat; product then frozen; consumer cooks fully
  • Oil management: Frying oil quality (colour, acid value, total polar compounds) monitored daily; degraded oil produces off-flavours and increased free fatty acids; discarded when TPC >25%
  • Allergen management on coating lines: Wheat (gluten), egg, milk common in batters; sesame increasingly common in specialty products; full allergen matrix must be maintained; changeover cleaning validated and swabbed before re-use for allergen-free product
Cook-Chill & Cooked Product Safety CCP
  • Core temperature CCP: All cooked-from-raw poultry must achieve ≥75°C at the geometric centre; this is the legal food safety CCP for Campylobacter and Salmonella destruction
  • Alternative equivalent: 70°C for 2 minutes (same log reduction of pathogens)
  • Temperature verified by calibrated penetration probe in oven validation studies and periodically in production
  • Oven types used in poultry further processing:
  • Spiral ovens — continuous throughput; impingement jet heating; common for large volume cooked products
  • Tunnel ovens — for breaded/battered products; multiple temperature zones
  • Steam injection ovens — for moist-heat applications; better yield retention
  • Rapid chilling after cooking: Cooked product must pass through the danger zone (63°C → 8°C) within 90 minutes (UK FSA/Chilled Food Association guidance); blast chilling required
  • Cook-chill shelf life: Typically 4–5 days at ≤5°C for simple cooked product; validated by challenge testing for Listeria monocytogenes growth; Technical team maintains shelf-life validation data
  • Listeria monocytogenes CCP in cooked product: Listeria can grow at refrigeration temperatures; post-process contamination risk from environment; environmental monitoring programme essential in cooked areas

Packaging Technologies
MAP, vacuum, gas flush, flow-wrap, tray-seal — how packaging choice determines shelf life, appearance, and retailer compliance
⚡
Must Know — Chapter 16
  • Gas flush (MAP): typically 70% O₂ / 30% CO₂ for fresh poultry retail trays
  • Gas flush for QSR bags: 100% CO₂ commonly used — bacteriostatic, extends shelf life
  • Vacuum packaging removes >99.9% O₂ — extends shelf life, causes colour change (purple meat)

Gas Flush / Modified Atmosphere Packaging (MAP) Process
  • MAP principle: Product sealed inside package with a controlled gas atmosphere replacing air; gas composition selected to inhibit microbial growth and/or maintain colour
  • Gas compositions for poultry:
  • Fresh retail (tray-seal): 70% O₂ / 30% CO₂ — high O₂ maintains oxygenated myoglobin (bright pink colour); CO₂ is bacteriostatic; shelf life typically 5–9 days chilled
  • QSR bulk bags (e.g., fresh portioned product): 100% CO₂ or N₂/CO₂ mix — no O₂ = no aerobic bacterial growth; shelf life extension; meat turns purple-red (acceptable as product is cooked before serving)

Foreign Body Detection CCP
  • Foreign body detector (metal detector) is a Critical Control Point (CCP) in HACCP
  • Minimum sensitivity (industry standard / BRCGS requirement):
  • Ferrous (Fe): 3.0mm sphere test piece
  • Non-ferrous (non-Fe, e.g., copper, aluminium): 4.5mm
  • Stainless steel (SS): 3.5mm — most difficult to detect due to low conductivity
  • Verification protocol: Test pieces passed through detector at start of shift, after every break, and end of shift; results recorded; if detector fails test → all product since last successful test quarantined and re-passed through working detector
  • Auto-reject: Detector must have automatic reject mechanism; no manual rejection; reject bin monitored for confirmed contaminants
  • X-ray systems: Higher-spec operations use X-ray in addition to or instead of metal detection; detects bone, stone, dense plastics, glass — but not all metals as effectively as metal detector; often used in combination
  • Foreign body incident response: Any confirmed foreign body in product → stop line → quarantine all product from last clear check → investigate source → correct → re-test → release or destroy; OV notified if metal body suggests equipment failure; customer notified if product has left factory

Chapter 18
Food Safety Management — HACCP & CCPs
The seven HACCP principles applied to poultry processing — hazard analysis, CCP identification, monitoring, verification, and documentation
⚡
Must Know — Chapter 18
  • HACCP = Hazard Analysis Critical Control Points — legal requirement under EC 852/2004 retained UK law
  • 7 HACCP Principles — must be applied in order; all documented
  • CCP = point where control is essential to prevent/eliminate/reduce hazard to acceptable level
  • OPRP = Operational Pre-Requisite Programme — controls significant hazard but at non-CCP points
  • CCPs in poultry processing typically: stunning, internal temperature (cooking), metal detection, chilling
  • CCP deviation = food safety incident; documented non-conformance and corrective action mandatory
  • HACCP plan must be reviewed: annually, after product/process change, after any food safety incident
  • BRCGS requires HACCP team to include multidisciplinary members — Technical, Production, Engineering, QA
The 7 HACCP Principles Legal Framework
  • Principle 1 — Conduct Hazard Analysis: Identify all biological (B), chemical (C), and physical (P) hazards at every process step; assess likelihood and severity; determine if significant
  • Biological: Campylobacter, Salmonella, Listeria, E.coli O157, Clostridium, Staphylococcus
  • Chemical: veterinary drug residues, cleaning chemical residues, lubricants, pesticides, allergens
  • Physical: metal fragments (equipment wear), bone shards, plastic, glass, wood, feathers
  • Principle 2 — Identify CCPs: Use decision tree to determine if each significant hazard is controlled at a CCP vs OPRP vs PRP
  • Principle 3 — Establish Critical Limits: The measurable boundary between acceptable and unacceptable at each CCP
  • Example: Chilling CCP — critical limit: product core ≤4°C; metal detection CCP — critical limit: all test pieces detected and auto-rejected
  • Principle 4 — Establish Monitoring Procedures: Who monitors, how, how often, and with what equipment — all documented
  • Principle 5 — Establish Corrective Actions: Pre-defined response to CCP deviation; product disposition; root cause investigation; re-verification
  • Principle 6 — Establish Verification Procedures: Confirm HACCP plan is working; internal audit, micro testing, calibration checks
  • Principle 7 — Establish Documentation & Record Keeping: All CCP monitoring records, deviations, corrective actions retained; minimum 2 years or shelf life +1 year

  • Pre-Requisite Programmes (PRPs): The foundational hygiene and operational controls that reduce overall contamination risk; not CCPs but essential to HACCP working
  • Cleaning and disinfection programme
  • Pest control programme
  • Personal hygiene (handwashing, PPE, illness policy)

  • Allergen management programme

Laboratory Methods — Core Techniques Lab Practice
  • Sample preparation:
  • Stomacher homogenisation: 25g sample + 225ml buffered peptone water (BPW); stomached 60 seconds → primary enrichment
  • Serial dilutions (10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁶) prepared in quarter-strength Ringer's solution for plating
  • TVC (ISO 4833): Plate Count Agar (PCA) incubated 30°C / 72 hours; colonies counted and expressed as cfu/g
  • Enterobacteriaceae (ISO 21528): Violet Red Bile Glucose (VRBG) agar; 37°C / 24 hours; pink/red colonies counted
  • E. coli (ECBS): Chromogenic agar (e.g., ChromoCult, Colilert); 37°C / 24 hours; characteristic blue/teal colonies
  • Salmonella (ISO 6579): Pre-enrichment (BPW 37°C/18h) → selective enrichment (RVS broth + MKTTn) → selective plating (XLD + Brilliant Green) → biochemical confirmation → serotyping
  • Campylobacter (ISO 10272): Selective enrichment (Preston broth 42°C/48h microaerophilic) → selective plating (modified CCDA/Preston agar 42°C/48h) → colony morphology + oxidase test + confirmation
  • Listeria (ISO 11290): Enrichment (Half Fraser → Fraser broth) → ALOA/Oxford agar plating → biochemical/PCR confirmation; turnaround 5–7 days; rapid PCR methods used for faster results
  • Rapid methods / PCR: VIDAS, ELISA-based, or PCR-based rapid methods used for Salmonella and Listeria screening; results in 24 hours; positive screens confirmed by conventional culture
  • Lab accreditation: UKAS ISO 17025 accreditation required for results used in regulatory compliance (FSA requirements, BRCGS); factory lab typically UKAS accredited for core methods or sends to accredited external laboratory (e.g., IFP Labs, Campden BRI)
Out-of-Limit (OOL) Results — Action Protocol Critical
  • Step 1 — Immediate notification: Lab technician or external lab notifies Technical Manager / QA Manager immediately on receipt of OOL result
  • Step 2 — Product traceability: Identify all batches produced from same period as sampled batch; apply hold to any product still in stock
  • Step 3 — Retain reference sample: Retain original sample; arrange re-test at same or different accredited lab to confirm result
  • Step 4 — Root cause investigation: Environmental swabbing; review CCP records; interview operative team; trace potential contamination source
  • Step 5 — Corrective action: Deep clean; chemical strip-down; re-sanitation; pest investigation if relevant
  • Step 6 — Customer notification: If product has left factory — notify customer immediately; provide batch/kill date details; support customer recall decision if required
  • Step 7 — Regulatory notification: Salmonella or Listeria in finished product may require FSA notification under Regulation (EC) 2073/2005 food safety criteria (retained UK law)
  • Step 8 — Re-verification: Re-test after corrective action; release product only when results confirmed acceptable; document all steps for audit trail

Nutritional Analysis Annual Requirement
  • Proximate analysis (Weende analysis): Standard nutritional composition determination
  • Moisture: oven drying at 103°C to constant weight; expressed as %
  • Crude protein: Kjeldahl or Dumas method; % nitrogen × 6.25 conversion factor = % protein
  • Total fat: Soxhlet extraction or NMR; expressed as %
  • Ash: incineration at 550°C; mineral content expressed as %
  • Carbohydrate: calculated by difference (100% minus moisture, protein, fat, ash)
  • Energy calculation: Atwater factors — protein: 4 kcal/g (17 kJ/g); fat: 9 kcal/g (37 kJ/g); carbohydrate: 4 kcal/g (17 kJ/g)
  • Salt calculation: Sodium content (by AAS or ICP-MS) × 2.5 = salt equivalent; declared on label as salt (g per 100g)
  • Saturated fatty acids: FAME (fatty acid methyl ester) analysis by GC; required for label declaration
  • Composite sample preparation (per QAP 8.3): 12 samples collected from 3 evenly-spaced points over 4 days production; composited and submitted; results versus reference: if 10% variance from previous analysis → investigate; 12-month re-analysis cycle mandatory
Physical & Chemical Parameters Testing Reference
  • pH: Measured using penetration pH probe directly into muscle; fresh broiler breast: 5.7–6.0 typical; thigh: 6.0–6.4
  • pH >6.3 in breast = PSE absent but may indicate DFD (poor ante-mortem conditions or microbial spoilage)
  • pH monitoring used in shelf-life studies — rising pH during storage indicates bacterial spoilage
  • Water Activity (Aw): Measure of free water available for microbial growth; raw poultry typically Aw 0.99+; relevant for marinated/brined/dried products
  • Aw <0.97 inhibits most bacteria; <0.95 inhibits Staphylococcus; <0.85 inhibits most moulds
  • Added water / water content: Gravimetric determination; relevant for marinated product; label declaration trigger if >5% added water by weight
  • Drip loss: Weight difference of vacuum-packed sample stored 24–48h at 4°C; expressed as % of initial weight; high drip loss = PSE meat or poor water-holding capacity; quality and yield metric
  • Colour (CIE L*a*b*): Instrumental colorimetry using Minolta or similar colorimeter; L* = lightness; a* = redness; b* = yellowness
  • PSE meat: high L* (pale), low a* (less red); DFD: low L* (dark), high a*
  • Used in QA monitoring of breast meat quality trends

  • Select 5 bags consecutively from production line
  • Open all 5 bags; remove all pieces; inspect each piece individually
  • Count total pieces; verify correct piece count and natural proportion (minimum 90% natural proportion)
  • Grade each piece: Green / Amber / Red / Critical using customer defect specification
  • Weigh each piece individually; verify against weight band specification
  • Record all findings on Rag Audit Sheet with time, line, product code, batch
  • Action triggers:
  • Any Red defect in 5-bag sample → count further 5 bags; if further Red found → quarantine all product since last acceptable check → rework or destroy
  • Any Critical defect → stop production immediately → quarantine entire production since last clear check → Technical manager notified → root cause investigation
  • Bag count below 98% accuracy → re-pack all held product → adjust bagging machine
  • Frequency: Minimum hourly; more frequently if defect trends developing or after machine adjustment
Product Hold & Release Procedure Critical
  • HOLD criteria (any of the following triggers an immediate hold):
  • OOL microbiological result on product from that production batch
  • Metal detector failure — all product since last passed test
  • Label error discovered — incorrect allergen, date, or product name
  • Temperature exceedance during production or storage
  • Critical quality defect (contamination, foreign body, gross mislabelling)
  • Customer complaint triggering investigation of batch
  • Physical HOLD process:
  • Product physically segregated — moved to designated hold area or labelled in-situ with red HOLD label
  • HOLD label: product code, batch, reason for hold, date/time, authorised by
  • Hold register updated immediately
  • RELEASE criteria: Technical manager reviews evidence (re-test result, root cause resolved, corrective action implemented); signs release authorisation; product returned to normal flow or destroyed
  • Destruction: Condemned product — weight recorded; witnessed destruction (rendering / ABP stream); disposal certificate retained
Sensory Evaluation (Shelf Life & Retention) Weekly
  • Retention samples: 1 pack per product per batch retained at factory throughout shelf life; stored at correct temperature; evaluated at end of shelf life
  • Sensory panel (per QAP 8.2): Pack retention sample assessed at end of shelf life; cook 1 batch per week; evaluate
  • Appearance: pack condition, colour of raw product, no slime or discolouration
  • Aroma: no off-odour (sour, sulphurous, putrid) — raw and cooked
  • Cooked texture: firmness, juiciness, no stringy or woody texture
  • Cooked flavour: no off-flavours, no rancidity, characteristic cooked chicken flavour
  • Mouthfeel: no gumminess, correct tenderness for product type
  • Sensory result trending: Results entered into MIMS database; any product failing sensory before end of shelf life = shelf life reduction investigation required; customer notified if commercial impact

  • Stage 1 — Concept & Brief: Customer or commercial team presents product concept; brief includes target flavour profile, packaging format, weight, price point, retail channel, target launch date
  • Stage 2 — Technical Feasibility: Technical team assess: raw material availability, line capability, allergen impact, regulatory compliance, shelf-life achievability; feasibility sign-off required before recipe development begins
  • Stage 3 — Kitchen Development: NPD kitchen produces bench-top samples; recipe iterations; internal sensory panel; cost modelling; ingredient specification sourcing
  • Stage 4 — Pilot Trial: Small-scale factory trial on production equipment; QA present throughout; CCP and OPRP behaviour assessed; packaging and labelling tested; yield measured

  • Product specification: ingredients, recipe, process parameters, packaging, labelling, weights, microbiological limits, nutritional composition, shelf life

  • Predictive microbiology: Software models (e.g., Pathogen Modelling Programme, ComBase) used to predict pathogen behaviour under proposed storage conditions; useful for initial shelf-life design
  • Challenge testing: Inoculation of product with target organisms (e.g., Listeria monocytogenes for RTE products) and storage under expected conditions; demonstrates organism does not grow to unsafe levels by use-by date
  • Durability studies: Real-time storage of product under worst-case conditions; microbiological and sensory monitoring throughout; results support use-by date declaration
  • Temperature abuse studies: Product stored above label temperature for defined periods; demonstrates safety margin exists; required for products likely to experience cold chain breaks in retail/foodservice
  • Recipe change shelf-life impact: Any change to salt, acid, water activity, or MAP gas composition may alter shelf life; re-validation required before label date change

Process Technology & Line Optimisation
Line speed, throughput metrics, OEE, yield optimisation, continuous improvement and the interface between Technical and Engineering
⚡
Must Know — Chapter 24
  • OEE (Overall Equipment Effectiveness) = Availability × Performance × Quality — the master production KPI
  • Line speed is measured in birds per hour (bph) — the primary throughput metric in primary processing
  • Any line speed change must be re-validated against CCP critical limits (e.g., bleed time, chilling time)
  • Preventive maintenance programme (PPM) prevents unplanned downtime — scheduled not reactive
  • CIP (Clean-In-Place) — automated cleaning system for tanks and enclosed lines; cycle validated chemically

  • Knife blade change frequency is a QA and food safety control — worn blades = poor cuts + metal risk
  • Engineering and Technical must communicate all process changes — undocumented change = audit failure
Line Speed & Throughput Production Metrics
  • Primary processing line speed: Measured in birds per hour (bph); typical range 6,000–15,000 bph in UK high-volume operations
  • Line speed governs: Bleed time (distance from neck cut to scalder ÷ line speed = seconds), scald dwell time, chilling carcass throughput, OV/MHI inspection rate
  • Legal inspection rate limit: EU Regulation (EC) 854/2004 sets maximum bird inspection rates for OV/MHI; line speed cannot exceed what allows full post-mortem inspection; OV has authority to require line slowdown
  • Line speed change process:
  • Technical manager + Production manager + Engineering sign off any speed change
  • CCP re-validation: bleed time, chilling capacity must be re-confirmed at new speed
  • HACCP plan reviewed; any new risk assessed
  • OV notified of speed change; OV confirms inspection capability maintained
  • Secondary processing throughput: Cut room capacity measured in kg/hour or bags/hour; cut room throughput must match primary line output to prevent bottlenecks and excessive holding of chilled carcasses
OEE & Continuous Improvement KPI
  • OEE = Availability (A) × Performance (P) × Quality (Q)
  • Availability: (Planned time − Downtime) ÷ Planned time; downtime includes breakdowns, changeovers, planned maintenance
  • Performance: (Actual output ÷ Theoretical output) × 100; measures speed losses and minor stoppages
  • Quality: (Good output ÷ Total output) × 100; measures product rejected or reworked
  • World-class OEE: >85%; typical food manufacturing: 60–75%
  • Continuous Improvement (CI) process:
  • Root cause analysis tools: 5-Why, Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagram, Pareto analysis
  • CI projects prioritised by defect frequency × cost impact
  • Engineering, Technical, and Production collaborative — change must be documented and validated
  • Preventive Maintenance Programme (PPM): Scheduled maintenance on all food contact equipment at defined intervals; maintenance records retained for audit; any repair on food contact equipment inspected by QA before line restart
  • Knife management programme: Knives and blades logged; maximum use period defined per knife type; worn knives withdrawn; broken blade = foreign body incident → full product check since last knife inspection
CIP — Clean In Place Systems Hygiene Technology
  • CIP applies to: Enclosed pipework, tanks (brine injection systems, marinade tanks), enclosed conveyor systems where manual cleaning is impractical
  • Standard CIP cycle:
  • Pre-rinse: cold water flush to remove bulk product residue
  • Caustic wash: hot sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution (1–2%, 70–80°C, 15–20 minutes) — removes protein and fat soils
  • Rinse: hot water flush to remove caustic
  • Acid wash: peracetic acid or citric acid rinse — removes mineral deposits, adjusts pH
  • Final rinse: potable water; effluent sampled to confirm no chemical residue
  • Disinfectant (optional): final sanitiser application; contact time before drain
  • CIP validation: ATP bioluminescence testing post-CIP; protein residue testing; microbiological verification swab; all results recorded and trended
  • Boiler house / hot water system: Provides hot water to CIP circuits; water softening plant prevents scale build-up in calorifiers; treated water quality monitored

Must Know — Chapter 27

Pest Control Programme Structure BRCGS + Legal
  • Pest Control Operator (PCO) contract:
  • External qualified company (e.g., specialist food industry contractors); BPCA (British Pest Control Association) membership required
  • Minimum visit frequency: monthly internal inspection + reactive visits
  • PCO technician holds site induction; familiar with food production areas; follows factory hygiene rules
  • Rodent control:
  • Bait stations: tamper-proof, numbered, mapped on site bait map; checked on each PCO visit; bait consumption recorded as activity indicator
  • Internal bait: non-toxic tracking devices inside production areas; external bait: toxic (rodenticide) bait in sealed stations around building perimeter
  • Electronic rodent monitoring: sensors in high-risk areas alert to activity in real time
  • Rodent activity evidence: droppings, gnaw marks, grease trails, runways; any evidence in food area → immediate corrective action; production suspended if evidence on food contact surfaces
  • Insect control:
  • EFKs (Electronic Fly Killers): installed at all external entry points and throughout production; UV light attracts flying insects; catch tray emptied and contents counted weekly
  • EFK data analysis: insect species and quantity trended by season; spike in Calliphoridae (blowflies) indicates possible carcass exposure; spike in stored product insects indicates raw material or packaging issue
  • Bait pots for crawling insects (cockroaches, beetles): food processing environments can harbour Tribolium (flour beetles) in dusty areas
  • Wild bird exclusion: All external vents, loading bay doors, overhead structures proofed against bird entry; bird netting at roof level; any bird in production area → stop production → clean → investigate entry point
  • Proofing inspection: Quarterly structural inspection with PCO; all gaps >6mm sealed; drainage points covered with stainless steel mesh; door seals inspected and replaced if worn
Listeria Environmental Monitoring Programme Critical
  • Why Listeria environmental monitoring is critical: L. monocytogenes survives and grows in cold, wet environments — exactly the conditions of poultry chilled production areas; once established in a niche (drain, floor crack, equipment seam), it can persist for years
  • High-risk Listeria harborage sites:
  • Floor drains: standing water, organic matter accumulation; primary reservoir
  • Floor/wall junctions: imperfect sealing allows organic matter to collect
  • Underside of conveyor belts and frames: difficult to clean; moisture trapped
  • Condensate drip points from refrigeration evaporator coils: cold moisture directly onto product contact areas
  • Hollow legs and frames of equipment: water traps inside tubular steel
  • Gaskets and seals on doors, hoppers, tanks: protein gets trapped in worn seals
  • Monitoring programme structure:
  • Environmental swabs monthly — 50+ sites per month across all chilled production areas
  • Positive result from non-product contact surface → deep clean + retest → if persistent positive → investigate equipment; replace gaskets; re-grout floor
  • Positive result from product contact surface → immediate production stop → full investigation → corrective action → verification testing before restart → notify Technical Director
  • Listeria species (not monocytogenes) positives still treated as indicator of hygiene failure requiring corrective action
  • Night hygiene team: Performs full factory clean between production shifts; separate team with dedicated supervisor; cleaning chemical concentrations verified by QA before hygiene sign-off; ATP bioluminescence test confirms clean

Traceability, Mass Balance & Recall
One step back, one step forward — the legal traceability requirement, mass balance testing, recall procedures and the 4-hour food safety response
⚡
Must Know — Chapter 30
  • Traceability law: EC 178/2002 (retained UK) — one step forward, one step back mandatory
  • BRCGS requires traceability exercise completed quarterly — within 4 hours for food safety incidents
  • Mass balance: weight of scheme-labelled product out ≤ weight of certified intake in
  • Recall vs withdrawal: Recall = product at consumer level; Withdrawal = product still in trade
  • Food safety recall must be notified to FSA within 24 hours of decision to recall
  • Batch code must link to: farm, kill date, line, shift, packaging run — full production chain
  • Recall plan must be tested annually — simulated exercise with timed traceability exercise
  • All despatch records retained minimum 2 years — essential for recall scope determination
Traceability System Legal — EC 178/2002
  • Legal requirement (EC 178/2002 Article 18, retained UK law): All food businesses must be able to identify: (a) all suppliers of any food ingredient and (b) all businesses to which they have supplied food
  • "One step back, one step forward" principle:
  • One step back: from any finished product batch → identify the raw material batch (farm, kill date, lot) that made it
  • One step forward: from any raw material batch → identify all finished products made with it and all customers who received those products
  • Poultry processing batch linkage:
  • Farm → AML/FID number → kill date → line → shift → chiller batch → cut batch → pack batch → case code → despatch note → customer delivery
  • Each link in this chain must be documentable with records available within 4 hours (BRCGS) or 24 hours (legal minimum)
  • Batch code design: Pack batch codes (kill date DD/MM/YY + shift code + line) allow any pack to be traced back to its specific farm load; BRCGS auditors test this with live traceability exercises
  • Traceability exercise (BRCGS requirement):
  • Quarterly test: select a random finished product code → trace back to raw material batch AND trace forward to all customer deliveries of that code
  • Mass balance check: weight of raw material received = weight of product made + weight of waste/by-product; discrepancy indicates traceability gap
  • Time limit: complete within 4 hours for food safety incidents; BRCGS tests this timing
Mass Balance — Scheme Compliance VACCP Control
  • Mass balance definition: Verification that the quantity of a claimed raw material matches the quantity of finished product carrying that claim; prevents fraudulent labelling of non-scheme product as scheme product
  • Red Tractor mass balance: Weight of Red Tractor-labelled finished product (kg) ≤ weight of certified Red Tractor intake birds (kg); any excess = potential fraud; investigated immediately
  • RSPCA Assured mass balance: Stricter; must balance to within acceptable rounding error (e.g., ±2%); audited at RSPCA Assured factory audit
  • Organic mass balance: Must be exact; certified organic live weight in = organic finished product out (minus measured yield losses); Soil Association audits this rigorously
  • Mass balance records:
  • Intake records: farm load weights, scheme certification, kill date
  • Production records: bags packed per SKU per shift; cased weight
  • Despatch records: scheme-labelled product quantity despatched per customer per period
  • Monthly reconciliation: Technical manager reviews and signs off mass balance monthly
Recall & Withdrawal Procedure Critical
  • Definitions:
  • Withdrawal: product still in the supply chain (warehouse, distribution, retail back room) — not yet with consumer; can be withdrawn without public communication
  • Recall: product has reached or is likely to have reached consumer — public communication required; FSA must be notified
  • Triggers for recall/withdrawal:
  • Confirmed pathogen (Salmonella, Listeria) in finished product reaching retail
  • Confirmed undeclared allergen on label (mislabelling)
  • Foreign body confirmed in consumer product (metal, glass, bone fragment)
  • Veterinary residue above MRL confirmed in product
  • Species substitution fraud
  • Recall process:
  • Step 1: Technical Director confirms recall decision — supported by investigation evidence
  • Step 2: Scope determined — all batch codes, date ranges, product types, all customers who received product
  • Step 3: Customers notified — written communication with product description, batch codes, reason, action required (return or destroy)
  • Step 4: FSA notified within 24 hours — via FSA RASFF (Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed) or direct notification; FSA may issue public warning
  • Step 5: Recall effectiveness check — verify all product accounted for; document quantities recalled vs quantities originally despatched
  • Step 6: Root cause investigation completed; corrective action implemented; evidence submitted to FSA
  • Step 7: Mock recall programme: minimum annual simulation; tested within 4-hour target; documented results reviewed and used to improve plan
  • Communication: All external recall communications reviewed by senior management and legal counsel before issue; do not speculate on cause in public communication; use FSA-approved communication template where available

Protein Plant & Effluent Water Treatment
Animal by-products processing, rendering, feather processing, and the multi-stage effluent treatment system — the environmental and regulatory obligations of a high-throughput poultry site
⚡
Must Know — Appendix A
  • ABP (Animal By-Products) regulations govern all non-food material from poultry processing
  • Category 3 ABP (feathers, blood, offal, condemned trim) — can go to pet food/rendering
  • Category 2 ABP (condemned carcasses, SRM equivalent) — rendering only; not pet food
  • Protein plant renders Category 3 ABP on site — feather meal, blood meal, meat-and-bone meal
  • Effluent from poultry processing: high BOD, suspended solids, fats, blood — cannot enter drain untreated
  • Consent to Discharge issued by EA (Environment Agency) — sets legal effluent quality limits
  • DAF (Dissolved Air Flotation) removes fats and suspended solids from process water
  • Aeration tanks biologically treat organic load; final effluent discharged to foul sewer or watercourse
Animal By-Products (ABP) — Regulatory Framework Legal
  • Regulation (EC) 1069/2009 (retained UK law as UK ABP Regulations): Governs collection, transport, storage, processing, and disposal of all animal by-products not intended for human consumption
  • Category 3 ABP (most relevant to poultry factory):
  • Feathers from healthy slaughtered birds
  • Blood, offal, feet, heads from healthy birds
  • Condemned trim and portions (QA rejects — not pathologically condemned)
  • Allowed use: pet food, rendering for feather meal/blood meal/animal fat, biogas
  • Category 2 ABP:
  • Pathologically condemned carcasses (disease, systemic infection)
  • DOAs
  • Must go to approved rendering only — not pet food; clearly segregated from Category 3
  • ABP records: Commercial documents accompany every ABP movement; records retained 2 years; APHA may inspect; any breach = criminal prosecution
  • Protein plant on site: High-throughput sites run on-site rendering operations; feathers processed through feather hydrolysis (autoclave under pressure — breaks down keratin); blood dried and processed; resulting materials (feather meal, blood meal, poultry by-product meal) sold as animal feed ingredients
  • Feather pit / offal pit / blood drainage: Collection points for by-product streams on kill line; segregated by category; pumped or conveyed to protein plant; temperature-controlled to prevent premature decomposition and odour
Effluent Treatment System Process
  • Why effluent treatment is critical: Poultry processing generates extremely high-strength wastewater; blood, fat, protein residues create very high BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand); direct discharge to watercourse without treatment = environmental offence + major fish kill risk
  • Effluent treatment stages (as found in high-volume poultry sites):
  • Stage 1 — Screening: Coarse and fine screens remove large solids (feathers, meat fragments) from process water; screened material sent to protein plant; prevents downstream equipment blockage
  • Crude pit: initial collection point for blood and high-strength effluent from kill line; crude pit motor house controls pumping to treatment
  • Stage 2 — Dissolved Air Flotation (DAF): Process water passed through DAF units; tiny air bubbles introduced under pressure; attach to fat and suspended solids; float to surface as sludge blanket; skimmed off
  • Chemical dosing: ferric sulphate (coagulant) + caustic (pH correction) dosed at mix chambers before DAF to improve fat/solids separation
  • DAF removes: fats, oils, suspended solids (blood cells, protein fragments); BOD reduced by 70–80%
  • DAF sludge: dewatered using decanter centrifuge; sludge cake sent to approved disposal (land spreading under Environment Agency approval or landfill)
  • Stage 3 — Balance tanks: Large holding tanks (hundreds to thousands of cubic metres) buffer flow and concentration variation; allow consistent feed rate to biological treatment
  • Stage 4 — Biological treatment (Aeration tanks): Remaining dissolved organic matter broken down by aerobic bacteria in aeration tanks; large surface area aerators or submersible mixers maintain oxygen supply
  • Activated sludge process: mixed liquor (effluent + bacteria) aerated; bacteria oxidise organic matter (BOD, ammonia); final effluent clarified in settlement tank
  • Anoxic tank: denitrification zone where nitrates are converted to nitrogen gas; reduces total nitrogen in final effluent — required by most EA discharge consents for poultry sites
  • Stage 5 — Polishing / Final Treatment: Final effluent quality checked against Consent to Discharge limits before discharge to foul sewer or controlled watercourse
  • Key parameters monitored: BOD (typically <30 mg/L consent limit), suspended solids (<35 mg/L), ammonia, pH, temperature
  • Nitrogen cryo tanks / liquid nitrogen: Some sites use liquid nitrogen for rapid product freezing (cryogenic tunnel freezers) or as inerting gas; separate delivery bay; trained operators only; asphyxiation hazard in enclosed spaces
  • Cooling towers: Reject heat from refrigeration condensers and process heat exchangers; Legionella risk — cooling tower water treated with biocide; Legionella risk assessment mandatory; quarterly monitoring
  • Reporting to Environment Agency: Self-monitoring of effluent quality required under Consent to Discharge; results reported to EA; any consent breach → EA notification → investigation → potential prosecution + civil penalty
Site Utility Systems Infrastructure
  • Boiler house: Generates steam for scalding, CIP hot water, and process heating; water treatment essential to prevent scale and corrosion in boiler tubes; legionella risk in hot water calorifiers
  • Refrigeration plant room: Primary refrigeration for all chilled production areas; typically ammonia (R717) refrigerant in large commercial food sites — extremely efficient but toxic; NH₃ leak detection systems mandatory; PPE (SCBA) available; emergency evacuation procedure documented
  • Compressed air: Used throughout the factory for pneumatic equipment, packaging machines, automated doors; food-grade air required for any compressed air in contact with product; oil-free compressors or in-line filtration; air quality tested quarterly
  • Water supply: Mains potable water supplemented by softened water (for boilers and CIP) and potentially borehole water (tested to potable standard); anti-backflow devices prevent process water returning to mains
  • Electrical systems: High-voltage transformer stations on site; restricted access; any electrical modification must not compromise food safety (e.g., cabling over exposed product areas)

CARCASE — Kill Line & Livestock System (Hellenic Systems)

2. Floor Hardware Integration — Bizerba BRAIN + BarTender + PLU Hardware Layer
Bizerba BRAIN — Hardware Middleware & Weighing Intelligence
  • What it is: Bizerba BRAIN (Business Resource and Automation Integration Network) is the middleware software layer that connects all physical weighing and labelling hardware to the factory IT network and MES/ERP systems
  • What hardware it connects:
  • Checkweighers — automated in-line scales that weigh every pack at production speed; BRAIN receives the weight data, compares against spec tolerances, triggers accept/reject signal
  • Weigh-price labellers — scales on the packing line that weigh individual packs and print a label with the actual weight and calculated price; BRAIN pushes the correct PLU (product parameters) to each scale terminal
  • X-ray inspection systems — BRAIN can receive pass/fail signals from X-ray units and log results against the product lot for traceability
  • Metal detectors — BRAIN interfaces with metal detector control units to log test results and detection events
  • Retail labelling printers — BRAIN routes print jobs to the correct printer for the correct SKU based on what is being produced at that moment
  • PLU management: BRAIN is the master repository for all PLU (Price Look-Up) data — product codes, descriptions, weights, pricing, label formats, allergen text; when a product changes (new weight, updated ingredient list, new use-by calculation), the change is made once in BRAIN and immediately propagates to all connected scales and printers on the line
  • Why it matters to QA/Technical:
  • Incorrect PLU = wrong label = allergen mislabelling risk or incorrect use-by date; BRAIN is therefore a critical control point for label accuracy
  • Any PLU change must be authorised by QA/Technical before being released in BRAIN; change control procedure documented and audited
  • BRAIN logs every label printed with its PLU, timestamp, and scale ID — this is the label audit trail used in complaint investigations

  • Bizerba hardware at factory level includes: TC series retail labelling scales, A series checkweighers, CWE-series in-motion checkweighers; all managed centrally through BRAIN
BarTender (Seagull Scientific)
  • Label design and print management software; acts as the execution bridge between FOPS8/BRAIN and the physical packaging line printers
  • Holds master label templates for every SKU; template locked and version-controlled — only authorised Technical users can edit master templates

  • Connects to barcode/QR printers (Zebra, SATO, Videojet); manages print queue, printer status, and reprint requests
  • Audit log of every label printed — what template, which data, which printer, which operator, at what time
  • BRCGS relevance: label audit trail from BarTender is primary evidence used during label accuracy investigations and customer complaints
PLU Databases
  • Master data repositories holding all product-specific parameters for every SKU produced on site
  • Parameters stored per PLU: product name (legal name), ingredient list, allergen flags, net weight, tare weight, pricing (where applicable), use-by calculation method, label template reference, customer code
  • PLU database is the single source of truth for label content — if the PLU is wrong, every label printed for that product is wrong
  • PLU setup and amendment is a Technical/QA-controlled process; requires dual authorisation (Technical + QA manager sign-off) before release to production
  • PLU records archived for minimum 2 years — required for historical label verification in complaint investigations

Chapter 01
Scope & Big Picture
Why the guideline exists, the core logic of shelf-life, and where each topic sits within G46
  • Why a 2nd edition: EC 2073/2005 microbiological criteria, HPA RTE guidelines, EU Listeria recommendations (EURL 2014), and the draft/new ISO challenge-test standard (ISO 20976-1:2019)
  • Aim: one harmonised testing protocol for the chilled industry (storage times and temperatures), unless an alternative is shown to be valid
  • Responsibility: the manufacturer decides shelf-life, usually with the retailer. No UK/EU law sets how long food should last, but there is specific guidance (e.g. vacuum-packed chilled foods, FSA 2017) and food must be fit for purpose (HPA 2009)
  • Core logic: shelf-life = the SHORTER of the safe-life and the acceptable quality-life
  • Safety tools: HACCP (always) plus microbiological risk assessment (MRA) and predictive modelling
  • Three deterioration types assessed: microbiological, biochemical, sensory

Chapter 02
Definitions & Date Labelling
Best before vs use by, FBO responsibility, exemptions, the 10-day rule, and food waste guidance
Core Definitions Legal
  • Shelf-life (IFST 1993): time after production during which food remains safe, retains desired sensory, chemical, physical and microbiological characteristics, and complies with label nutritional declarations when stored as recommended. Also called "date of minimum durability"
  • Never longer than the safe-life or the acceptable quality-life

Responsibility & Exemptions Compliance
Responsibility & Rules
  • The Food Business Operator (FBO), usually the manufacturer, assigns best before or use by, documented within the HACCP-based food safety system (FSAI 2017)
  • FBOs who break down bulk product and repack it become responsible for the new shelf-life (Reg. (EU) 1169/2011)
  • Use by vs best before decided with the Defra 2011 decision tree; some chilled foods (e.g. yogurt) can carry best before if assessed as no safety risk
  • FBOs must not alter dates in a way that misleads the consumer; food past use by is unsafe
  • Frozen meat, meat preparations and unprocessed fishery products need a "frozen on" date (day, month, year, uncoded)
Foods Exempt from a Shelf-Life Date (Reg. 1169/2011)
  • Fresh fruit/veg not washed, cut or peeled (seed sprouts still need a date)
  • Wines and similar; drinks over 10% ABV
  • Bakers'/pastry cooks' wares normally eaten within 24 hours
  • Vinegar, cooking salt, solid sugar, sugar confectionery, chewing gum
  • Non-prepacked or loose food, food packed on premises at consumer's request
  • Meat frozen in bulk for further handling — only a production date needed
WIP & the 10-Day Rule
  • Total life = ingredient life + WIP life + product life
  • FSA 2017: chilled MAP or vacuum-packed foods should not exceed 10 days (non-proteolytic C. botulinum) unless controlling factors are in place
  • Exemption: heat treatment of at least 90°C for 10 minutes (or equivalent) applied to WIP, or another controlling factor
Food Waste (WRAP)
  • Give consumers as much life as safely possible (longer after-opening life, longer best before on frozen)
  • Donating food past best before is acceptable — quality issue, not safety
  • Use by food can be redistributed/donated if frozen by midnight on the use by date, then thawed, cooked immediately and used within 24 hours (not for RTE foods that will not be cooked)

Factors That Influence Shelf-Life
Hurdle technology and the seventeen variables that push a product toward its end-point
  • Key idea: effects act on microbiological, chemical and physical parameters, usually ending in lost sensory quality. The end-point is where change is noticeable or unacceptable; the time taken to reach it is what must be determined
  • Hurdle technology: pH, aw, preservatives, temperature etc. are each non-lethal alone but combine (often synergistically) to stop growth. Every hurdle must hit target in every batch

Evaluation Method Chapter 06 — The Shelf-Life Evaluation Sequence Shelf-Life Study Notes
Chapter 06
The Shelf-Life Evaluation Sequence
Stage I kitchen trials, Stage II factory scale-up, and open-life testing for multi-use packs
  • Two stages for new products: Stage I kitchen/pilot scale, Stage II full-scale factory trials and ongoing production
  • Existing products: if the sequence was never done, or the product is modified, start at Stage II with a full HACCP or MRA
  • Minor formulation differences between kitchen and factory can change shelf-life
  • Do not taste samples until safety is established. Document and retain all information for audits
Figure 1 — Full Shelf-Life Evaluation Sequence (G46)
Stage I — Kitchen or Pilot Scale (Commercial Viability Only)
  • 1. Product concept: set target shelf-life early; consider end use (single/multi-use, home freezing)
  • 2. Define product and process characteristics: ingredients, processing, packaging, pH, aw
  • 3. Preliminary HACCP: paper data to assess pathogen risk (Listeria, Salmonella, S. aureus, B. cereus, Clostridium)
  • 4. Make samples: ingredients, heat treatment and cooling profile as close as possible to full scale
  • 5. Preliminary assessments: realistic portions, packed and stored at least to target life; monitor sensory and spoilage growth
  • 6. Target achieved? No: reconsider product/process concept. Yes: proceed to Stage II
Figure 2 — Preliminary Shelf-Life Evaluation (Stage I)
Stage II — Factory Trials & Ongoing Production
  • 1. Review HACCP using final formulation, processing and packaging; redo if parameters change
  • 2. Scale-up issues: pre-production runs verify the marketing brief, confirm processing parameters, assess shelf-life
  • 3. Shelf-life evaluation: real-time testing with samples made by routine procedures, stored like retail distribution
  • 4. Choose sampling frequency, then tests (see Chapter 08)
  • 5. Interpret data: maximum safe life, maximum quality life — do both meet the target?
  • 6. Set working shelf-life.
  • 7. Ongoing monitoring and periodic review
Figure 3 — Continuation of Shelf-Life Evaluation (Stage II)
Open Life (Multi-Use Products)
  • Test after opening, mimicking consumer handling (open pack, remove portion, store remainder, sample at realistic points)
  • MAP cooked meats may change pH and flora after opening
  • If freezable, establish life after thawing

  • Time split guidance: FSAI 2019 suggests two-thirds of life for manufacture/distribution/display, one-third for consumer. EURL 2014: same up to 21 days; over 21 days use 7 days for manufacture/distribution, then 50/50 retail/consumer
Frozen & Freeze-Thaw
  • Use -18°C. Biglia et al. 2018: 745 domestic freezers, mean -20.3°C (range -5.6 to -37°C); 73% of fridge-freezers below -18°C
  • Growth stops below -2°C but freezing does not destroy organisms; Campylobacter declines
  • Freeze-thaw: evaluate time of freezing and life once thawed — many organisms resume growth on defrost
Ambient & Hot/Humid Export
  • 21–25°C typical UK trials. UK average air temperature ~10°C (summer to just over 18°C)
  • Kitchens: mean 18.5°C, max 29.6°C. RH 70–90%
  • Hot/humid export: e.g. 30°C at 80% RH or 37°C at 20% RH
Rationale Behind the Chilled Protocol Appendix III
  • Manufacturer chillers: spec 0–4°C; 5 chillers averaged 2.6–2.7°C with peaks 7.2–7.5°C during defrost
  • Typical dwell: 1–2 days for most chilled foods; sandwiches/sushi dispatch within hours
  • Transport: vehicle temperature 0–4°C, below 4 or below 5°C; if above 5°C check between packs
  • Retail (EFSA Listeria baseline, UK): 75–85% of samples at 5°C or lower; 90% at 6°C; 95% at 7°C; 99% at 9°C
  • Consumer transport (WRAP 2010): after abuse (1h at 20°C + 1h at 30°C) sliced ham reached 18.6°C, still 10.5°C 5 hours later. 45% of households take ~30 min to get home — hence 2h at 22°C
  • Domestic fridges: Biglia 2018 (671 fridges) mean 5.3°C, range -4.1 to 14.3°C; ~13% of fridge-freezers and 24% of ice-box fridges above 8°C. Loading with shopping raises mean by almost 2°C
  • Law and mishandling: Consumer Rights Act 2015 and EC 2073/2005 require food safety criteria met under reasonably foreseeable conditions — allow for abuse; a pack labelled 5°C may reasonably end up at 8°C
Evaluation Method Chapter 08 — Sampling, Tests & Laboratory Shelf-Life Study Notes
Chapter 08
Sampling, Tests & Laboratory
Sample numbers, timing, and the four test domains — microbiology, chemistry, sensory, laboratory accreditation
  • Sample at the start of life, at target shelf-life, and at least 3 occasions between
  • Test beyond target too — extra samples must be stored
  • Minimum triplicate packs each time; single/duplicate results are less reliable
  • Use samples from at least 3 pre-production runs; repeat trials to reflect variability
  • Record critical parameters (pH, aw, salt) to confirm within HACCP limits
  • Multi-component products: total life = shortest component life
  • Include after-opening life for multi-use products

Moulds Various mould species (Aspergillus, Penicillium, Fusarium and others) Multicellular filamentous fungi. Grow as visible colonies — the "furry" growth seen on spoiled food. Some produce mycotoxins. Mainly an ingredient and air quality concern. Mould in pre-chill or ingredient packing areas = product contamination risk. High mould counts in dry ingredients (breadcrumbs, stuffing, spices) indicates poor storage or moisture ingress. Air plates: same limits as Yeasts above (<10 acceptable, >20 adverse). Dry intake tested per batch vs specification. MF / TP4109 (same code as Yeasts in some suites)
Bacillus spp. (non-cereus) Bacillus subtilis, B. licheniformis, B. pumilus, B. megaterium etc. Gram-positive, spore-forming aerobic rods. Very heat-resistant spores. Widespread in soil and the environment. Found in ingredients (especially stuffing, spices, breadcrumbs) and occasionally finished products. High counts in stuffing = ingredient quality issue. Not pathogenic (unless confirmed as B. cereus), but cause spoilage and product quality failures. Must be speciated if above limit. If above unacceptable limit: speciate to confirm species. If B. cereus detected — further differentiation from B. thuringiensis required before action. Reported in identification columns (ID / Cspp)
Sulphite Reducing Clostridia (SRC) Anaerobic, spore-forming, Gram-positive rods. Reduce sulphite compounds as part of their metabolism. Found in soil and intestinal tracts. Used only in water testing — specifically for water that originates from or is influenced by surface water. Their presence indicates the water source has been contaminated with soil or faecal material at some point. Customer-specific action if detected. Water: 0 cfu/100ml — customer specific action if detected. SRClW / TP4143 (water only)
